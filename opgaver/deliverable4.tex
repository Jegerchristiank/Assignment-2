\subsection{Deliverable 4: Reflections on the Modelling Process}
\label{sec:deliverable4}

\subsubsection{Question 1: Data Missed or Misrepresented in the Interview-Based Model}
The interview captured the intended routine, but missed practical micro-actions and recovery behavior. In observation, extra coordination and workaround actions appeared, and one intended activity (seated breakfast plus schedule review) did not occur as a distinct step. The interview therefore represented baseline intent well, but underrepresented adaptation under time and resource constraints.

\subsubsection{Question 2: What Counted as an Activity in Each Model}
In Deliverable 1, an activity is an intention-level block. In Deliverable 2, an activity is an observable timestamped event. This granularity difference explains part of the mismatch: one interview activity can map to several observed events.

\subsubsection{Question 3: How the Event Log Constrained What Could Be Modelled}
The event log supports strong sequence and timing analysis, but weakly captures motives and tacit reasoning. It shows what happened and when, but not reliably why behavior changed unless context notes are added.

\subsubsection{Question 4: Potential and Limits of Automatic Event Capture}
Some events could be captured automatically (alarm/app checks), while others are difficult to capture meaningfully (searching for missing items, informal coordination). Automatic capture improves temporal coverage, but does not replace qualitative interpretation \parenciteshort{guidehealthinformatics2026ch27,zajac2026methodsknowing}.

\subsubsection{Question 5: Variations and Exceptions Revealed by Observation}
Observation revealed concrete variation that the interview treated as optional: sequence changes, omitted intended steps, and workaround actions. This is the core value of observation in say-do studies: it reveals work-as-done under real constraints.

\subsubsection{Question 6: Relevance of Qualitative Discovery in Process Modelling}
Qualitative discovery is relevant because interview and observation provide complementary evidence. The interview model captures knowledge-as-stated; the observation captures knowledge-as-discovered. Following Chapter 27, model quality improves when elicitation and validation are iterative rather than one-off \parenciteshort{guidehealthinformatics2026ch27}.
