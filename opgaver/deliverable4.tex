\subsection{Deliverable 4: Reflections on the Modelling Process}
\label{sec:deliverable4}

\paragraph*{\textbf{Task Requirement (Deliverable 4)}}
\textit{Reflect on six modelling questions: data missed/misrepresented, activity definition differences, event-log constraints, automation potential, variations revealed by observation, and why qualitative discovery matters for process modelling.}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Question 1:} \textit{What kinds of data were missed or misrepresented in the interview-based model and why?}  
\textbf{Answer:} 
The interview model captured routine intent, but missed situated details that appeared in observation: micro-coordination (E03), recovery behavior (E10), and omission of the planned seated breakfast-and-schedule step. This pattern is expected in \textit{manual knowledge acquisition}: \textit{interviews} primarily capture recalled intent, while \textit{observation} captures situated adaptation \parenciteshort{guidehealthinformatics2026ch27,zajac2026methodsknowing}. In this case, the interview model therefore overestimated routine linearity.

\item \textbf{Question 2:} \textit{What counts as an activity in each model, and how did this differ?}  
\textbf{Answer:} 
In Deliverable 1, the \textit{interview-based DCR} uses intention-level activities (A01-A09). In Deliverable 2, the event log (the empirical basis for the \textit{observed DCR}) uses timestamped observable actions. This granularity mismatch means one DCR activity can map to multiple logged events. The same routine can therefore appear stable at intention level but variable at event level.

\item \textbf{Question 3:} \textit{How did the event log constrain what could be modelled?}  
\textbf{Answer:} 
The event log supports strong analysis of order, timing, and deviations (\textit{work-as-recorded}), but it captures motives and judgement only indirectly. Without contextual notes, causal interpretation of why a deviation occurred remains weak. This is why descriptive/reflexive note separation was retained in the method and documented in Appendix~\ref{app:part1_supporting}, Table~\ref{tab:d2_observation_guide}.

\item \textbf{Question 4:} \textit{Do you think that the event log could be captured automatically by a system? Why or why not?}  
\textbf{Answer:} 
Automatic capture is strong for digital traces (alarm, app checks, timestamps), but weak for tacit coordination, local workarounds, and searches for missing items. In Chapter 27 terms, automated traces should be combined with \textit{qualitative discovery} methods when the task includes social and contextual adaptation \parenciteshort{guidehealthinformatics2026ch27,zajac2026methodsknowing}.

\item \textbf{Question 5:} \textit{How did the observation help uncover variations or exceptions that were not mentioned in the interview?}  
\textbf{Answer:} 
Observation revealed concrete \textit{exceptions}: changed order (E07 before E09), omitted planned step (A06 not distinct), and workaround behavior (E10). Applied to this case, Chapter 27 implies that process evidence should not only test conformance but also explain recurring exceptions that may be locally rational \parenciteshort{guidehealthinformatics2026ch27}.

\item \textbf{Question 6:} \textit{Why is it relevant to consider qualitative methods of discovery (e.g., observations) when building process models?}  
\textbf{Answer:} 
For this routine, model quality improved by triangulating \textit{knowledge-as-stated} (interview + transcript), \textit{knowledge-as-discovered} (observation), and \textit{work-as-recorded} (event log). The resulting model is more realistic than any single method alone, consistent with Chapter 27's iterative \textit{knowledge acquisition} approach \parenciteshort{guidehealthinformatics2026ch27}.
\end{enumerate}
