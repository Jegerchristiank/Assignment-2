\subsection{Deliverable 4: Reflections on the Modelling Process}
\label{sec:deliverable4}

\subsubsection{Question 1: Data Missed or Misrepresented in the Interview-Based Model}
The interview model captured the intended flow well. However, it missed small but important actions and made the routine look more stable than it was. E03 (roommate messaging) and E10 (borrowing a lock) were not modelled as explicit steps, and A06 (seated breakfast plus schedule review) did not appear as a distinct observed event. This happened because the interview focused on the ``typical'' sequence and only briefly mentioned variation (Appendix~\ref{app:part1_transcript}, L14-L16, L18). The result is a useful knowledge-as-stated baseline, but not a full model of work-as-done.

\subsubsection{Question 2: What Counted as an Activity in Each Model}
In the interview model, an activity is an intention block (A01-A09). In the observation log, an activity is a timestamped event unit (E01-E14). This granularity difference matters. For example, ``toilet and shower'' is one interview step, while wake-up phone checks and short coordination actions become separate events in observation. The interview model is better for communicating the routine, while the event log is better for analysing order, timing, and deviations. If these two levels are treated as identical, conformance errors will be inflated by modelling choices rather than real process problems.

\subsubsection{Question 3: How the Event Log Constrained What Could Be Modelled}
The event log made sequence and timing comparison easy. At the same time, it limited interpretation of motives. It shows what happened and when, but usually not why, unless this is written in \texttt{context\_note}. D06 is a clear example: the log shows a breakfast change, but not whether it was caused by preference, time pressure, or missing resources. Event logs are therefore strong for trace structure and weaker for tacit reasoning.

\subsubsection{Question 4: Potential and Limits of Automatic Event Capture}
Some parts of this routine could be captured automatically, especially digital touchpoints such as alarm events, app checks, and transport lookup. Other parts are harder to capture in a useful way, for example bathroom tasks, searching for missing items, and informal recovery coordination. Even when a message is logged, its process meaning still requires interpretation. Automatic capture improves temporal coverage, but it cannot replace qualitative interpretation. A practical approach is to combine passive logs with short contextual annotations \parenciteshort{guidehealthinformatics2026ch27,zajac2026methodsknowing}.

\subsubsection{Question 5: Variations and Exceptions Revealed by Observation}
Observation revealed concrete exceptions that the interview had framed only as possibilities. The seated breakfast was skipped, packing moved in the sequence, and a recovery step was needed when a lock was missing. These changes were triggered by local constraints such as available food, missing items, and incoming communication. They were still necessary to complete the routine on time. This is where observation contributes most: it shows how the process stays functional under variation, not only how it is ideally described.

\subsubsection{Question 6: Relevance of Qualitative Discovery in Process Modelling}
Qualitative discovery is relevant because interview and observation provide different but complementary knowledge. In this case, the interview model acts as interview-based DCR (knowledge-as-stated), while the observed log acts as observed DCR (knowledge-as-discovered). The delta analysis shows that both views are valid, and that neither is complete on its own. Following Chapter 27, model quality improves when elicitation and validation are treated as iterative discovery steps, not one-time extraction \parenciteshort{guidehealthinformatics2026ch27}. In this study, the combined method produced a model that is both communicable and empirically tested.
